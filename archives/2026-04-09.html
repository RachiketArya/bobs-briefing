Anthropic Built a Model Too Dangerous to Release: Claude Mythos Preview

This is the story everyone is talking about this morning. Anthropic announced Tuesday — and the analysis is flooding in all week — that Claude Mythos Preview is so capable at finding high-severity vulnerabilities in operating systems and browsers that they will not make it generally available. Under Project Glasswing, the model has been quietly released only to a handful of vetted tech companies. The numbers are frightening and impressive in equal measure: 93.9% on SWE-bench Verified, 94.6% on GPQA Diamond, and during internal red-teaming it uncovered thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities across every major OS and browser. Anthropic framed the decision as its Responsible Scaling Policy working as designed — the first time a frontier lab has declined a broad release purely on offensive cyber grounds. For reference, OpenAI's GPT-2 was called "too dangerous" in 2019; this is several orders of magnitude more consequential.

🏦 Aspire Relevance
This is the exact scenario your AI governance playbook needs to contemplate. Two implications for Aspire: (1) Third-party model risk — if a lab can hold a model back for safety reasons, vendor contracts and model approval workflows must account for sudden capability shifts and restricted-access tiers. (2) Offensive-capability disclosure — MAS's FEAT principles and the new AI Risk Management Guidelines for FIs don't yet explicitly cover dual-use cyber capabilities, but regulators will notice this. Expect questions in your next MAS touchpoint about how Aspire evaluates frontier models before adoption. Worth drafting a one-page internal position now.

💰 Business

Perplexity Revenue Jumps 50% in a Month After Pivoting to AI Agents

The Financial Times broke the story yesterday and it's reverberating today: Perplexity's ARR has crossed $450 million as of March, up roughly 50% in a single month, as the company aggressively pivoted from answer engine to autonomous agent orchestration layer. Revenue has now doubled in a single quarter. The shift is architectural: instead of asking Perplexity for answers, users assign outcomes — book the flight, draft the memo, chase the invoice — and an agent handles the full workflow with usage-based pricing. The company has been pushing Comet (its agentic browser) hard, and the monetization flywheel appears to have clicked. This is the first concrete datapoint we have that "agents as a product category" can actually scale revenue at velocity.

🏦 Aspire Relevance
Usage-based pricing for agents that execute actions on behalf of users is highly relevant to anything Aspire ships in the accounts payable, expense, or bookkeeping flows. The lesson from Perplexity is that the willingness to pay jumps when the product stops answering questions and starts completing tasks. If Aspire has a "copilot that drafts" today, the next curve is an "agent that does" — and the monetization can follow the same usage-based model. Worth benchmarking against your current product roadmap.

Goodbye, Llama? Meta Launches Muse Spark — First Proprietary Model from Superintelligence Labs

Meta dropped a surprise yesterday: Muse Spark, its first proprietary (read: not open-weight) model since Mark Zuckerberg restructured the AI org into Meta Superintelligence Labs. The pitch is efficiency: Meta claims Muse Spark matches or beats Llama 4 Maverick on reasoning benchmarks while using more than an order of magnitude less compute. The move marks a quiet but dramatic strategic pivot — for years Meta has owned the "open-weight champion" narrative. Now it is building behind closed doors for its own products first, with Llama presumably continuing as a second-tier offering. Analysts are already asking whether this means the end of Meta's strongest differentiator in the AI race.

⚖️ Regulation

MAS AI Risk Management Guidelines for FIs: Why This Matters Now More Than Ever

With Anthropic's Mythos news today, the Monetary Authority of Singapore's proposed Guidelines on AI Risk Management for Financial Institutions suddenly feel less like policy theater and more like a necessary operating manual. The guidelines apply to all FIs in Singapore and codify MAS's expectations on: board-level oversight of AI risk, model lifecycle controls (from data sourcing through deployment and monitoring), explicit policies on third-party AI models, and the capabilities/capacity FIs need to actually do all this. They build on the FEAT Principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability, Transparency) that MAS co-created with the industry. For any FI operating in Singapore, this is the framework regulators will measure you against — whether or not it becomes law in its current form.

🏦 Aspire Relevance
This is literally your job, Rachiket. The MAS guidelines are the single most relevant regulatory document for your Head of AI role at Aspire. Three practical hooks: (1) build a Model Risk Management Register mapped to the MAS lifecycle stages; (2) designate clear AI accountable persons at the MD/SVP level — MAS loves a named owner; (3) define tiered approval thresholds for model deployment based on customer impact (a pricing model > an internal summarizer). Mapping Aspire's current AI use cases to FEAT + the new Guidelines is a 1–2 week exercise that will pay dividends in every future regulatory conversation.
